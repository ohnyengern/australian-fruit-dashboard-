Australian Fruit Production

Eight selected fruits: from harvest size to market value.All years refer to years ending June.

01

A snapshot of eight selected fruits

A few fruits account for most of the harvest. Oranges, bananas and apples supplied almost 64% of the combined 2025 tonnage. Changes in these large crops therefore carry more weight in the total than equally large percentage changes in smaller crops.

Yet harvest size tells only the opening chapter. Strawberries and blueberries together supplied just 5.5% of tonnage, but a small harvest can still generate substantial production value. The charts that follow separate physical scale, growth and economic contribution.

02

How fruit production has changed over time

The combined harvest grew by 14.3%, but growth was uneven. Oranges contributed approximately 41% of the net increase between 2021 and 2025. Their large starting volume meant that steady expansion added more tonnes than faster growth in smaller crops.

Banana production fell over the same period, partly offsetting gains elsewhere. Meanwhile, avocados peaked in 2024 before declining in 2025. A larger final harvest can therefore conceal quite different paths: sustained growth, recovery or a recent setback.

A rebound does not necessarily recover an earlier loss. Table-grape production fell 19.9% in 2024, then rose 22.6% in 2025. Despite the larger percentage gain, the 2025 harvest remained 1.8% below its 2023 level because recovery began from a smaller base.

By contrast, mandarins and oranges grew in every annual comparison. This separates consistent expansion from recovery after a weak year. Read each colour against the previous harvest: annual percentage changes describe momentum, while the line chart shows the underlying production level.

Stable rankings can hide substantial changes in production. Bananas remained second even as their harvest declined, while avocados stayed sixth despite growing 68.3% between 2021 and 2025. Rank describes relative position; it does not reveal the size or direction of change.

Mandarins moved ahead of table grapes in 2024 and remained ahead in 2025. Both fruits finished above their 2021 production levels, so this was not simply one crop replacing another. Their changing positions reflect different growth paths within an expanding combined harvest.

The fastest-growing crop did not add the most tonnes. Avocados expanded by 68.3%, adding 53,300 tonnes. Oranges grew by 20.9% but added 98,843 tonnes, almost twice as much. Percentage growth and additional tonnage answer different questions about the changing harvest.

Mandarins gained 74,446 tonnes, turning a 32,349-tonne deficit behind table grapes into an 11,604-tonne lead. Bananas lost 34,281 tonnes but remained a major crop. Starting size matters: rapid expansion can leave a small crop below a larger, declining one.

National supply can depend heavily on one state. Queensland supplied about 94% of bananas in 2025, while New South Wales contributed approximately 62% of oranges. For these crops, the national harvest is closely tied to production in a particular region.

Apples had a broader production base: Victoria supplied approximately 46%, with the other states collectively contributing the majority. A high growth rate need not mean a large contribution. South Australian avocado production rose from 800 to 4,261 tonnes, an increase of 433%. Western Australia added 23,342 tonnes, far more, despite its lower percentage increase of 172%.

Tasmania began at zero and reached 17 tonnes, so no percentage change can be calculated. These cases explain why the growth map complements the symbol map: colour reveals relative change, while circle area reveals scale. Missing data also differs from a recorded zero.

04

The value behind fruit production

Tonnage alone is a poor guide to production value. Table grapes generated approximately A$3,822 per tonne, about four times oranges' A$946. Oranges supplied 29% of the selected harvest by weight but approximately 11% of its combined production value.

Blueberries illustrate the contrast particularly clearly: their small harvest generated substantial value relative to its weight. These ratios describe production value per tonne, not retail prices or growers' profit. A higher-value crop is not necessarily more profitable, because production costs are not shown.

05

From Australian farms to the world

Different fruits depend on different outlets. Around 62% of the combined harvest entered the domestic fresh market. Yet table grapes sent approximately 61% of their own production overseas, making export demand far more important to table grapes than bananas.

Processing absorbed a further 16% of the combined harvest. Oranges and apples supplied approximately 95% of this volume, connecting these crops with products beyond fresh fruit. The national market split therefore conceals very different roles for domestic sales, exports and processing within each crop.

Export exposure differs by fruit and destination. Among the named markets shown, China received substantial shipments of table grapes, mandarins and oranges. Papua New Guinea was the largest named destination for apples, demonstrating that nearby Pacific markets also matter to particular crops.

The map shows selected major destinations rather than every export market; shipments grouped as “Others” are omitted. Use the selector to compare patterns, but read arrow thickness against the common scale. Similar-looking routes do not necessarily represent similar volumes or the same share of exports.

Three crops accounted for about 81% of the export-value increase. Table grapes, mandarins and avocados added a combined A$266.2 million between 2021 and 2025. Together, they explain most of the A$330 million increase across the eight selected fruits.

This is a change in export value, not export tonnage. The chart alone cannot separate changes in volume, prices or product mix. Read alongside the production charts, it completes the story: a crop's contribution depends on its scale, growth and market destination.
